# Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

## Metadata and contribution

- **Title:** Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models
- **Authors:** Jason Wei, Xuezhi Wang, Dale Schuurmans, Maarten Bosma, Fei Xia, Ed Chi, Quoc V. Le, Denny Zhou
- **Year:** 2022
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2022
- **arXiv:** 2201.11903
- **Survey role:** P0 foundation for explicit intermediate reasoning and later agent planning/reflection methods

## Research problem

The paper asks whether a language model can solve multi-step reasoning tasks more reliably when a few-shot prompt demonstrates not only the final answer but also a sequence of intermediate natural-language reasoning steps. Standard few-shot prompting maps an input directly to an output. **Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting** instead supplies exemplars of the form input → intermediate reasoning chain → answer and asks the model to generate a similar chain before its final prediction.

The method requires no gradient update or task-specific fine-tuning. Its central empirical claim is not that CoT universally improves every model/task, but that **the benefit emerges strongly at sufficiently large model scale and on tasks requiring multiple reasoning steps**.

## Experimental coverage

The authors evaluate CoT prompting across three broad families:

1. **Arithmetic reasoning:** GSM8K, SVAMP, ASDiv, AQuA, MAWPS and related arithmetic sets.
2. **Commonsense reasoning:** CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA, date understanding, sports understanding, and SayCan-style language understanding/planning questions.
3. **Symbolic reasoning:** last-letter concatenation and coin-flip state tracking, including out-of-distribution sequence lengths.

Most arithmetic experiments use **eight manually written chain-of-thought exemplars**. The model families span GPT-3/InstructGPT from hundreds of millions to 175B parameters, LaMDA up to 137B, PaLM up to 540B, UL2-20B, and Codex. This scale sweep is essential to the paper's conclusion because small models often gain little or even degrade with CoT.

## Main arithmetic results

The clearest results occur on GSM8K, where the problems require several arithmetic steps. For **PaLM 540B**, standard prompting scores **17.9%**, while chain-of-thought prompting reaches **56.9%**, a **+39.0 percentage-point** gain. GPT-3 175B rises from **15.6% to 46.9%**, and Codex from **19.7% to 63.1%**.

PaLM 540B also improves on the other principal arithmetic benchmarks:

- **SVAMP:** 69.4 → **79.0** (+9.6).
- **ASDiv:** 72.1 → **73.9** (+1.8).
- **AQuA:** 25.2 → **35.8** (+10.6).
- **MAWPS:** 79.2 → **93.3** (+14.2).

The variation is informative. Gains are largest when the task genuinely requires decomposition and much smaller on easier or more one-step problems. On the MultiArith subset, for example, PaLM 540B improves from roughly **42.2% to 94.7%**, whereas some simple MAWPS subsets show much smaller gains. The authors therefore argue that CoT is particularly useful when ordinary scaling of direct-answer prompting has begun to plateau on genuinely compositional tasks.

## Scale-dependent emergence

Across model families, CoT performance often remains flat or worse than standard prompting for small models and then improves sharply around very large scales (roughly the 100B-parameter regime in the models studied). The generated chains from small models are frequently fluent but illogical. Larger models produce substantially more coherent intermediate steps.

This is an important limitation on how the paper should be cited: CoT is not demonstrated as a model-agnostic recipe. Its effectiveness depends jointly on model capacity, task complexity, and prompt design.

## Commonsense results

CoT also helps on several commonsense tasks, though not uniformly. For PaLM 540B, the reported standard → CoT results include:

- **CommonsenseQA:** 78.1 → **79.9** (small gain).
- **StrategyQA:** 68.6 → **77.8**.
- **Date understanding:** 49.0 → **65.3**.
- **Sports understanding:** 80.5 → **95.4**.
- **SayCan-style task:** 80.8 → **91.7**.

The small CommonsenseQA improvement reinforces the authors' point that CoT helps most when the problem benefits from an explicit sequence of intermediate inferences rather than simple factual or local classification.

## Symbolic and length-generalization results

The symbolic tasks show particularly strong length generalization. On **last-letter concatenation**, PaLM 540B improves from **7.6% to 99.4%** for the two-word in-distribution setting. For longer out-of-distribution sequences, standard prompting is nearly zero while CoT remains high: approximately **0.2% → 94.8%** for three words and **0% → 63.0%** for four words.

On the **coin-flip** task, PaLM 540B moves from 98.1% to **100%** in distribution, from **49.3% to 98.6%** at length three, and from **54.8% to 90.2%** at length four. This supports the interpretation that an explicit intermediate state representation can help the model extrapolate a repeated reasoning procedure beyond the sequence lengths demonstrated in the prompt.

## Robustness to exemplars and annotators

The authors test alternative chain-of-thought exemplars written by different annotators and variations in prompt construction. CoT generally remains better than standard prompting, especially at large scale, but the exact result can vary materially with the exemplar set. In a LaMDA-137B coin-flip robustness study, one annotator's prompt reaches **99.6%**, whereas another reaches only about **71.4%**, with the direct-answer baseline around 50%.

The correct interpretation is therefore that CoT's effect is robust enough to reproduce across many prompt sets but **not prompt-invariant**. A reviewer should not read the paper as proving that any arbitrary chain of reasoning will help equally.

## Error analysis

The appendix manually categorizes **45 errors from PaLM 62B** into approximately **20 semantic-understanding errors, 18 missing-step errors, and 7 other errors**. Scaling to PaLM 540B resolves only part of each class: about 6/20 semantic errors, 12/18 missing-step errors, and 4/7 other errors are corrected.

This suggests that scale plus CoT is especially effective at recovering omitted intermediate operations, but it does not eliminate semantic misunderstanding. For agent systems, this distinction matters because a verbose trajectory can still be based on an incorrect interpretation of the environment or goal.

## Critical assessment and limitations

The paper is foundational, but several limits are important for an agent survey:

- **Generated chains are not guaranteed to be faithful explanations of the model's internal computation.** The paper demonstrates a useful output format, not transparent access to neural reasoning.
- **Reasoning chains can be wrong even when the final answer is right, and vice versa.** Later verification methods are needed if a system relies on intermediate steps as evidence.
- Manual chain-of-thought exemplars add annotation cost; using large quantities of such traces for fine-tuning can be expensive.
- CoT is sensitive to model scale and prompt choice, so improvements are not guaranteed for smaller models or easy/non-compositional tasks.
- Natural-language reasoning does not by itself guarantee factual grounding, tool correctness, or safe action execution.
- The benchmark tasks are mostly static QA/reasoning problems. They do not test partial observability, browser state changes, action side effects, recovery, or long-horizon web interaction.

## Relevance to web agents and schema-guided extraction

For this survey, the paper should be used as a **reasoning foundation**, not as direct evidence of web-agent performance. It established that large models can benefit from explicitly decomposing a problem into intermediate steps, which later agent methods adapt into plan generation, scratchpads, ReAct-style reasoning/action traces, reflection, tree search, and self-correction.

The key transfer to web automation is conceptual: a browser agent often must decompose a goal into subgoals, maintain intermediate state, and revise a plan. However, CoT alone does not solve grounding or execution. A web agent can generate a plausible reasoning chain and still click the wrong element, omit records, lose provenance, or take an unsafe action. Evaluation must therefore keep reasoning quality separate from task success, field correctness, grounding, provenance, and safety.
