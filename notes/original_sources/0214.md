## Analysis

<!-- note-body-start -->
### Research problem and contribution

This survey treats games as controllable interactive testbeds for language-enabled agents. It proposes a single-agent architecture built from memory, reasoning, and perception–action interfaces; a multi-agent view organized around communication and organizational structure; and a challenge-centered taxonomy across action, adventure, role-playing, strategy, simulation, sandbox, and diverse game settings.

### Method and evidence base

The paper is an analytical narrative survey rather than a formal systematic review. It synthesizes methods for working and long-term memory, reflection and world-model construction, instruction-guided and fine-tuned reasoning, multimodal perception, hierarchical action, inter-agent communication, topology, and role allocation. It then connects architectural choices to genre-specific requirements such as low-latency control, partial observability, opponent modeling, long-horizon exploration, persistent social state, and open-ended goal formation. Its benchmark table summarizes environment modality, player mode, and availability rather than combining performance scores.

### Limitations and critical assessment

The latest retrieved manuscript is arXiv v5 dated June 2026, so its scope and wording are newer than the register's original 2024 record; citations should identify the version actually used. The paper does not give a reproducible search string, database list, screening flow, or study-quality rubric. Its genre categories overlap, and game-specific abstractions do not map cleanly to live web constraints such as authentication, changing content, user privacy, or irreversible transactions. Many claimed emergent multi-agent phenomena depend on prompts, roles, and environment rules chosen by designers, and the paper appropriately notes the lack of open, reproducible large-scale platforms. Benchmark success/win rates are too coarse to localize memory, reasoning, perception, or coordination failures, while human/LLM judgments of believability and role fidelity introduce subjectivity.

### Relevance to the survey

The paper is useful for comparing closed interactive environments with web-agent benchmarks. It supports persistent world-model memory, feedback-grounded reasoning, hierarchical action control, and diagnostic component-level evaluation. Game results should not be treated as direct evidence of real-web reliability, but the controlled settings offer design patterns for testing long horizons, hidden state, and multi-agent coordination safely.
