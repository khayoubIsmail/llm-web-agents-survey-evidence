# Agent-RewardBench: Towards a Unified Benchmark for Reward Modeling across Perception, Planning, and Safety in Real-World Multimodal Agents

## Metadata and scope

- **Authors:** Tianyi Men, Zhuoran Jin, Pengfei Cao, Yubo Chen, Kang Liu, Jun Zhao
- **Venue:** Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, Volume 1: Long Papers
- **Pages:** 17521–17541
- **DOI:** 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.857
- **arXiv:** 2506.21252
- **Survey role:** S5.1 benchmark/evaluation evidence; P2 supporting source

## Research problem

The paper studies a different evaluation target from ordinary agent benchmarks: whether a multimodal model can act as a **reward model for agent trajectories**. In a multi-step agent task, a reward model should judge whether an intermediate action or response is better or worse, so its feedback can support training, search, backtracking, and self-correction. The authors argue that reward-model benchmarks designed for chat, mathematics, or retrieval do not test the perception, planning, and safety judgments required by multimodal agents operating in web and embodied environments.

## Benchmark design

Agent-RewardBench contains **1,136 verified pairwise samples** organized across **3 dimensions** and **7 agent scenarios**:

- **Perception:** web perception and embodied perception.
- **Planning:** web navigation, embodied planning, and travel planning.
- **Safety:** web-attack safety and embodied safety.

The seven scenarios span web, mobile/desktop grounding, Minecraft/embodied tasks, autonomous driving, virtual-house tasks, travel planning, and safety-sensitive interactions. The response pool is generated from **10 diverse MLLMs**, covering commercial/black-box models and open white-box models.

The final benchmark distribution is explicit: 136 Web Perception samples (11.97%), 163 Embodied Perception (14.34%), 259 Web Planning (22.80%), 247 Embodied Planning (21.74%), 149 Travel Planning (13.12%), 100 Web Safety (8.80%), and 82 Embodied Safety (7.22%), totaling 1,136 samples.

## Data sources

The benchmark reuses established task sources rather than inventing all agent situations from scratch. Web perception is drawn from SeeClick; embodied perception from MFE-ETP. Planning uses Mind2Web for web navigation, PCA for embodied domains such as Minecraft, autonomous driving, and virtual homes, and TravelPlanner for constrained itinerary planning. Web-safety cases use pop-up attack scenarios, while embodied safety uses MSSBench.

Before final filtering, the authors report selecting 204 web-perception and 278 embodied-perception items; 417 Mind2Web planning samples, 317 embodied-planning samples, 180 travel-planning samples, 100 web-attack samples, and 186 embodied-safety samples as initial sources.

## Pair construction and quality control

The construction pipeline has four stages: **response generation, data pairing, difficulty control, and manual verification**. Five black-box and five white-box MLLMs generate responses for intermediate agent steps. Positive and negative responses are paired; instances that are entirely correct or entirely incorrect are removed. The authors sample ten candidate pairs per query.

Difficulty is then controlled with three smaller multimodal models—Pixtral-12B-2409, LLaVA-OneVision-Qwen2-7B, and InternVL2-8B. Pair order is reversed to reduce position bias, and medium/harder discriminative cases are retained. Finally, **three AI graduate students** manually inspect the controlled data. The paper reports **1,443 entries before the two filtering rounds and 1,136 after filtering**.

## Evaluation protocol

The reward model receives a question plus a pair consisting of a chosen and rejected response and must select the better one. Every pair is evaluated twice with swapped answer order to mitigate positional bias. Temperature is set to 0, and the final score averages the two orderings. Results are aggregated for perception, planning, safety, and an overall average.

## Main quantitative results

The benchmark is difficult even for leading commercial MLLMs. In Table 2, **Gemini-1.5-Pro has the best overall average, 61.6**, followed closely by **GPT-4o at 61.4**; Claude-3.5-Sonnet scores 57.9 overall. Capability strength does not translate cleanly to safety: GPT-4o records 65.9 perception and 73.2 planning but only **39.2 safety**, while Claude-3.5-Sonnet records 73.3 perception and 71.2 planning but only **22.4 safety**. Gemini-1.5-Pro obtains 73.4 perception, 69.6 planning, and 37.7 safety.

The open-model results also show that scale helps perception/planning but does not solve reward modeling. Qwen2-VL-72B scores 69.1 on perception and 60.1 on planning, compared with 57.5 and 51.8 for Qwen2-VL-7B. Llama-3.2-11B-Vision scores 53.5 perception and 50.6 planning. These are substantive benchmark findings absent from the previous generic note.

## Downstream relevance to agent search

The authors test whether benchmark reward accuracy predicts useful agent-search performance. On a random **100-sample VisualWebArena subset**, GPT-4o generates trajectories and multiple reward models guide A* search. The reported relationship between Agent-RewardBench accuracy and downstream VisualWebArena search accuracy has **Pearson r = 0.981 with p = 0.003**. The paper also reports a no-search baseline around **34% accuracy**, supporting the claim that reward-model choice materially affects reward-guided search.

This analysis is important for agent evaluation because it connects an offline pairwise reward benchmark with an actual interactive downstream use case rather than treating preference accuracy as an isolated score.

## Error analysis

For planning errors, the authors manually annotate **50 negative samples** and identify six categories: understanding error, missing steps, hallucination, repeated steps, unclear next action, and wrong action. This provides a useful failure taxonomy for trajectory-level evaluators. It also shows why final-task success alone cannot tell whether an evaluator can identify the specific intermediate failure that should trigger correction or backtracking.

## Critical assessment and limitations

Agent-RewardBench contributes a useful step-level perspective that complements ordinary end-to-end agent benchmarks. For web automation, a reward model that can distinguish a good from bad intermediate click/plan can support search or recovery even when the base agent initially fails.

However, the paper has several limitations that should constrain survey claims:

- The benchmark is assembled from existing public datasets/environments, so the authors explicitly acknowledge potential **data contamination/pollution**.
- Positive labels remain tied to the source datasets' annotations; errors or simplifications in those sources can propagate into the reward benchmark.
- Pairwise chosen-versus-rejected classification is easier to score than open-ended trajectory evaluation and may not capture calibration of reward magnitude.
- Many scenarios are sampled subsets of existing environments rather than complete live deployments, so the benchmark does not itself prove reward-model reliability under arbitrary web change.
- The strong VisualWebArena correlation uses only five reward models and a 100-item downstream subset, so r=0.981 should be treated as promising validation rather than universal evidence that benchmark ranking will transfer to every agent domain.

## Relevance to this survey

This paper supports three survey points. First, **agent evaluation should operate below final task success**, because step-level rewards reveal errors in perception, planning, and safety. Second, **safety evaluation cannot be inferred from general model strength**: the strongest perception/planning models still show weak safety-reward accuracy. Third, evaluation artifacts can have downstream operational value when they guide tree search, backtracking, or correction.

For WADE-style evaluation, Agent-RewardBench is especially relevant to trajectory/process metrics and recovery evaluation. Its separation of perception, planning, safety, and step-level judgments complements field correctness, provenance, safety, and task success rather than replacing them.
