# Evaluation and Benchmarking of LLM Agents: A Survey

## Metadata and scope

- **Title:** Evaluation and Benchmarking of LLM Agents: A Survey
- **Authors:** Mahmoud Mohammadi, Yipeng Li, Jane Lo, Wendy Yip
- **Year:** 2025
- **Venue:** KDD '25, Toronto, Canada, August 3–7, 2025
- **Length:** 11 pages
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3711896.3736570
- **arXiv:** 2507.21504
- **Survey role:** S5.1 evaluation taxonomy and benchmark/metric synthesis; P2 secondary source

## Research problem and contribution

The paper argues that evaluating an LLM agent is qualitatively different from evaluating an isolated language model or deterministic software. Agents operate in dynamic environments, plan over multiple steps, call tools, retain memory, interact with users or other agents, and can cause external state changes. A single answer-quality score is therefore insufficient.

Its main contribution is a **two-dimensional taxonomy** for organizing agent evaluation. The first dimension, **Evaluation Objectives**, asks *what* aspect of an agent is being evaluated. The second, **Evaluation Process**, asks *how* the evaluation is performed. The paper also identifies enterprise-specific evaluation requirements that are often absent from academic benchmarks.

## Taxonomy: Evaluation Objectives

The paper divides evaluation objectives into four major categories:

1. **Agent Behavior** — user-facing outcomes such as task completion, output quality, latency, and cost.
2. **Agent Capabilities** — process-level competencies including tool use, planning/reasoning, memory/context retention, and multi-agent collaboration.
3. **Reliability** — consistency across repeated runs and robustness to changed inputs, environments, or tool failures.
4. **Safety and Alignment** — fairness, harmful behavior, transparency, policy adherence, privacy, and related trustworthiness concerns.

This distinction is useful for the survey because a web agent can achieve a final task while still be poor in grounding, tool selection, trajectory efficiency, robustness, provenance, or safety. Conversely, component metrics can diagnose where a failed end-to-end task broke down.

## Concrete metrics synthesized by the survey

For **task completion**, the paper catalogs Success Rate / Task Success Rate / Overall Success Rate, Task Goal Completion, Pass Rate, binary goal rewards, and repeated-trial measures such as **pass@k** and **pass^k**.

For **latency and cost**, it distinguishes Time To First Token for synchronous interaction from end-to-end request latency, and treats token consumption as a practical cost proxy.

For **tool use**, the survey identifies a more granular evaluation chain than simple task success:

- **Invocation Accuracy** — whether a tool should be called at all.
- **Tool Selection Accuracy** — whether the correct tool is selected.
- **Retrieval Accuracy / rank accuracy@k** — whether the correct tool can be found in a large repository.
- **MRR and NDCG** — ranking quality for retrieved tools.
- **Parameter-name F1 / parameter correctness** — whether required arguments and values are generated correctly.
- **AST-based validation** — syntax/structure of a tool call.
- **Execution-based evaluation** — whether the call actually executes with the intended outcome, addressing semantic errors that syntactic validation can miss.

For **planning and reasoning**, it discusses **Node F1** for selecting required tools, **Edge F1** or **Normalized Edit Distance** for invocation order/structure, T-Eval's next-tool reasoning metric, AgentBoard **Progress Rate**, program-similarity measures, and **Step Success Rate** for executed plans.

For **memory**, it distinguishes memory span and memory form and points to long-dialogue evaluations ranging from **40+ turns** to **600+ turns**. Representative metrics include factual recall accuracy and consistency across turns.

## Reliability and robustness

The paper makes an important distinction between *capability* and *reliability*. **pass@k** rewards succeeding at least once across repeated attempts, whereas **pass^k** requires success across all k attempts and is therefore more appropriate when consistent behavior matters. It cites the tau-benchmark as an example of the stricter consistency framing in retail and airline-booking tasks.

Robustness is evaluated through perturbed instructions, irrelevant or misleading context, typos/dialects, changed environments, and injected tool failures. The survey cites WebLinX for dynamic web-page changes and ToolEmu for tool-error resilience. This is directly relevant to generalized web automation, where DOM/layout changes and unexpected API/tool outputs are normal rather than exceptional.

## Taxonomy: Evaluation Process

The second taxonomy dimension covers the mechanics of evaluation:

- **Interaction Mode:** static/fixed-input evaluation versus interactive evaluation with users or environments.
- **Evaluation Data:** synthetic or real-world datasets and domain-specific benchmarks.
- **Metrics Computation Methods:** deterministic code/rule-based scoring, LLM-as-a-Judge / Agent-as-a-Judge, and human-in-the-loop assessment.
- **Evaluation Tooling:** frameworks and operational infrastructure for repeatable, continuous evaluation.
- **Evaluation Contexts:** controlled simulations, mocked APIs/sandboxes, web simulators, and progressively more realistic deployment settings.

The survey notes a fundamental trade-off in evaluation context: more realistic environments provide stronger ecological validity but are generally more costly, variable, and potentially less secure; controlled mocks are reproducible but can underrepresent production behavior.

## Metric-computation trade-offs

**Code-based evaluation** is deterministic, objective, and reproducible for structured outputs but poorly suited to open-ended or subjective responses. **LLM-as-a-Judge** scales to nuanced qualitative criteria but introduces model-based judgment dependence; the paper also discusses Agent-as-a-Judge variants. **Human-in-the-loop evaluation** remains the strongest reference for subjective quality and safety-critical judgments, but is expensive, slow, and difficult to scale.

For an evidence framework such as WADE-Eval, this supports combining deterministic schema/state checks with semantic/judgment-based metrics rather than treating one scoring mode as sufficient for every target.

## Evaluation tooling and contexts

The paper connects evaluation to Evaluation-Driven Development and continuous agent monitoring. It mentions OpenAI Evals, DeepEval, InspectAI, Phoenix, Galileo, and platform-level evaluation features, alongside an AgentOps architecture for deployment-time monitoring.

Evaluation contexts range from mocked APIs and sandboxes to web simulators such as **MiniWoB, WebShop, and WebArena**, and eventually live deployment. The paper's framing is useful for distinguishing benchmark reproducibility from real-world generalization.

## Enterprise-specific evaluation challenges

The paper identifies four enterprise concerns that substantially extend ordinary benchmark success:

1. **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC):** agents acting for different users must obey permission boundaries; evaluation should test access-control-sensitive tasks.
2. **Reliability guarantees:** production systems need repeatable behavior, not occasional success, which requires repeated trials and creates evaluation cost.
3. **Dynamic and long-horizon interactions:** short episodes miss performance drift, context retention, and cumulative consequences. The paper cites multi-day agent simulations and 600-turn conversational-memory evaluation as examples of longer horizons.
4. **Domain-specific policies and compliance:** task success alone is insufficient if an agent violates approval workflows, retention rules, quotas, GDPR/HIPAA-style constraints, or confidential-data boundaries.

## Future research directions

The authors propose four main directions:

- **Holistic evaluation frameworks** that assess interdependent competencies rather than isolated dimensions.
- **More realistic evaluation settings**, including enterprise-style multi-user interaction, RBAC, and domain knowledge.
- **Automated and scalable evaluation**, including synthetic test generation, simulators, and LLM/agent-based judging.
- **Time- and cost-bounded protocols** that make repeated and long-horizon evaluation feasible during development.

These directions are summarized as a need for evaluation that is simultaneously holistic, realistic, scalable, and efficient.

## Critical assessment and limitations

This is a **secondary survey**, not a new benchmark experiment. It should therefore be used to support taxonomy, terminology, metric organization, and cross-benchmark comparison, while empirical performance claims should preferentially cite the underlying primary benchmark papers.

The taxonomy is broad and useful but necessarily compresses heterogeneous evaluation settings. Some categories overlap in practice—for example, tool-use correctness affects both capability and final behavior, while safety/compliance can also be treated as a task constraint. The survey also reflects the benchmark/tool landscape available by mid-2025 and will date quickly in a rapidly evolving field.

A further strength is that the final published metadata is clear: the paper is not merely an arXiv preprint as the old note claimed. It appeared in KDD '25 with DOI `10.1145/3711896.3736570` and should be cited as the archival version.

## Relevance to this survey

The paper is especially useful for S5.1 because it provides a principled reason not to equate **task success** with **complete agent quality**. For generalized web automation and schema-guided extraction, evaluation needs to separate final success from tool/action correctness, planning progress, grounding, memory, reliability, safety/compliance, latency/cost, and the realism of the evaluation context.

It also helps justify WADE-style multi-metric evaluation: deterministic state/schema/provenance checks can coexist with trajectory, semantic, reliability, cost, and safety measures rather than being collapsed into one benchmark score.
