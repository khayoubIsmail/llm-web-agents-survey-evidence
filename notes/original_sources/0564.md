# Watch Every Step LLM Agent Learning via Iterative Step-Level Process Refinement

## Research problem and contribution

Iterative step-level Process Refinement (IPR) addresses the weakness of training agents only from final task outcomes. It estimates an action's process reward by Monte Carlo rollouts from that step, contrasts a model-generated continuation with the expert action/continuation, and trains on both step-level and trajectory-level preferences.

## Method and evaluation

The pipeline first supervised-fine-tunes a Llama-2-7B agent on GPT-4-generated expert trajectories. It then samples five continuations from each candidate step to estimate expected outcome reward, constructs preferred/rejected action continuations when their reward gap crosses a threshold, and iteratively optimizes outcome-DPO, step-DPO, and SFT losses. Evaluation uses WebShop, InterCodeSQL, and ALFWorld. IPR reaches average reward 69.4 versus 66.4 for the strongest outcome-refinement baseline ETO, with gains of 5.8, 7.2, 2.5, and 3.2 points on WebShop, InterCodeSQL, ALFWorld-seen, and ALFWorld-unseen. Ablations support both DPO components, and the sampled step-order labels reach up to 82% agreement with a WebShop heuristic scorer.

## Limitations and critical assessment

Monte Carlo process rewards inherit the base scorer's competence and the environment's terminal reward, so they are noisy proxies rather than direct labels of action correctness. Expert-trajectory anchoring constrains exploration and may miss alternative successful strategies. The iterative preference loop can overfit limited self-generated data; the learned reward model is tested mainly within one task and underperforms fresh rollouts. IPR also requires substantially more sampling and training—reported as roughly three times ETO training duration in one comparison—and its three benchmarks do not test live-web non-stationarity or safety-critical actions.

## Relevance to the survey

IPR supports step-level supervision for training web agents and shows why outcome-only success signals are too sparse to localize errors. It is best cited as an offline refinement technique whose reward quality and compute cost must be audited, not as proof that model-generated process labels are intrinsically trustworthy.
