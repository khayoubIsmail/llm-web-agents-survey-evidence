# ToolSword: Unveiling Safety Issues of Large Language Models in Tool Learning Across Three Stages

## Research problem and contribution

ToolSword studies safety failures introduced by tool learning itself. It organizes the tool-use pipeline into input, execution, and output stages and defines two scenarios per stage: malicious queries and jailbreak attacks; noise misdirection and risky cues; and harmful feedback and error conflicts. The resulting benchmark contains 440 samples involving 100 constructed tools.

## Method and evaluation

Eleven open and closed models are tested using their native tool-call or prompt formats at temperature zero. Input-stage attack success rate measures failure to reject unsafe requests, execution-stage tool-selection error measures susceptibility to misleading or risky tool descriptions, and output-stage tests measure propagation of harmful or factually conflicting feedback. Even GPT-4 has a 63.64% attack success rate on unmodified malicious queries, while models specialized for tool use often execute nearly every such request. In harmful-feedback tests, the average unsafe-output rate is 65.45%, whereas human raters reject all harmful feedback. The paper also compares standard dialogue with tool-learning contexts and finds that tool context can weaken otherwise visible safety alignment.

## Limitations and critical assessment

The scenarios are controlled and largely isolate one stage at a time, so they do not capture compounding failures in a live multi-step task or an adversary that adapts after observing the agent. Hand-built tools and 55-item scenario subsets support diagnosis but limit ecological coverage. Safety metrics mostly count rejection or error events and do not grade consequence severity or whether a cautious response still completes the legitimate part of a task. The work identifies vulnerabilities but does not implement or evaluate a defense; the proposed remedies are future directions. Model versions and tool APIs may also change, reducing the durability of absolute rankings.

## Relevance to the survey

ToolSword provides a useful lifecycle taxonomy for web-agent safety: harmful intent can enter with the user, tool metadata can misdirect selection, and untrusted tool output can corrupt the final answer. It supports treating tool descriptions and tool responses as distinct trust boundaries rather than assuming that model-level refusal training governs the entire agent loop.
