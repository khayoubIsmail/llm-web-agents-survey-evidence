## Core contribution

Yu et al. propose **TrustAgent**, a survey framework for trustworthy LLM agents and multi-agent systems. Its main value for this survey is that it does not organize the literature only as a list of threats. It decomposes trustworthiness simultaneously along three views:

1. **Modular view:** intrinsic components (brain, memory, tools) and extrinsic interactions (agent–agent, agent–environment, agent–user).
2. **Technical view:** attacks, defenses, and evaluation.
3. **Trust-dimension view:** safety, privacy, truthfulness, fairness, and robustness.

This makes the paper a direct trust-taxonomy comparator for Cluster D and a relevant secondary source for the manuscript's security/governance synthesis.

## Detailed synthesis

### Intrinsic trust: brain

The survey treats the LLM "brain" as vulnerable to jailbreaks, prompt injection, backdoors, hallucination-related truthfulness failures, and other alignment weaknesses. It groups countermeasures around alignment, filtering/detection, and shield or multi-agent mechanisms, then distinguishes targeted safety/trust assessments from broader agent benchmarks. The important conceptual point is that trust is not a single binary property of the model; different failure modes require different evaluation objects and controls.

### Intrinsic trust: memory

For memory, the survey identifies poisoning, privacy leakage, and misuse of stored information as major risks. The reviewed defenses include detection, prompt/context modification, and output intervention. A particularly relevant limitation is the lack of a systematic, reliable evaluation framework dedicated to agent memory. This supports treating persistent state as a separate trust boundary rather than as an implementation detail.

### Intrinsic trust: tools

Tool-integrated agents face both manipulation of tool use and unsafe or abusive tool execution. The survey notes that dedicated defenses for tools are less developed than attack studies and discusses evaluation through datasets and sandboxed settings. It also points to compound risks in tool chains, where the output or compromise of one component can affect later actions.

### Extrinsic trust: agent-to-agent interaction

The multi-agent section covers cooperative or infectious attacks, propagation through communication, and defenses based on collaboration or topology. The survey emphasizes that failures can spread through agent interactions rather than remaining local to one model. Dedicated evaluation of these propagation effects remains limited.

### Extrinsic trust: agent-to-environment interaction

The environment is treated as both a source of observations and a source of risk. The survey covers physical and digital environments, including Web/networked settings, healthcare, finance, and social environments. Environmental feedback can mislead an agent, and existing evaluations are fragmented across domains. This is relevant to Web extraction because page content is simultaneously task evidence and an untrusted input channel.

### Extrinsic trust: agent-to-user interaction

The user-facing discussion covers trust calibration, transparency, personalized behavior, privacy, and oversight. A trustworthy agent should neither overstate confidence nor hide consequential uncertainty. Human control therefore remains part of the trust boundary for high-impact or ambiguous tasks.

### Five trust dimensions

Appendix A makes the cross-cutting dimensions explicit:

- **Safety:** avoiding harmful behavior and unsafe consequences.
- **Privacy:** protecting sensitive information and controlling disclosure.
- **Truthfulness:** producing information that is accurate and not misleading.
- **Fairness:** avoiding unjustified discriminatory behavior.
- **Robustness:** maintaining reliable behavior under perturbation, attack, or distribution shift.

Appendix B then maps a broad literature into the paper's combined taxonomy.

## Evidence relevant to the manuscript

### Cluster D related-work table

This paper fills the specific conceptual gap between **threat-enumeration surveys** and **trust-decomposition surveys**. It is therefore suitable as a third Cluster D row alongside the generalizability survey and the computer-use safety/security survey.

### Section 8.4: output integrity and provenance

The modular and trust-dimension views support an end-to-end conception of trust: risk can enter through the model, memory, tools, other agents, the environment, or user interaction. However, the paper does **not** operationalize extraction-specific field integrity, source-linked provenance, record association, or silent dataset corruption. This boundary is important for the manuscript's argument: action-level safety or generic trust controls can coexist with an incorrect extracted value.

### Section 8.5: governance

The survey supports the claim that governance should span several trust dimensions and several system components rather than rely only on a security-threat checklist. It is useful as a secondary organizing source, while quantitative attack/defense claims should still be supported by primary empirical studies.

## Limitations and boundaries

- The work is a **survey**, not a new empirical computer-use or Web-agent system.
- Its scope is broad LLM agents and multi-agent systems rather than generalized Web automation or schema-guided extraction.
- It does not define an extraction-specific evidence object or field-level provenance contract.
- It does not provide a single end-to-end benchmark for silent data corruption in Web extraction.
- Some primary works discussed by the survey are preprints; the survey itself is an archival KDD 2025 publication.
- The three taxonomy views are conceptual organization; they should not be misrepresented as an empirical guarantee that a system satisfying one dimension is trustworthy overall.

## Synthesis statement

Yu et al. provide an archival 2025 trust survey that organizes LLM-agent trustworthiness across system modules, technical roles, and five explicit trust dimensions. This makes it a strong Cluster D comparator and supports end-to-end security/governance reasoning, while leaving open the extraction-specific problem of verifying that every committed field is source-supported, correctly associated, and resistant to silent corruption.
