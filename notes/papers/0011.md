### Paper 10 — Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners

#### Paper contribution

Kojima et al. introduce **Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (Zero-shot-CoT)**, showing that a simple natural-language trigger such as “Let’s think step by step” can elicit multi-step reasoning from sufficiently large pretrained language models without task-specific chain-of-thought demonstrations.

#### Method

The procedure has two prompt stages:

1. **Reasoning extraction:** append a generic reasoning trigger to the task question so the model generates a chain of thought.
2. **Answer extraction:** feed the generated reasoning back with an answer-format cue such as “Therefore, the answer is …”.

Experiments use greedy decoding for the core zero-shot comparisons and evaluate arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense/logical reasoning datasets. The main study uses InstructGPT variants, with key results reproduced using PaLM and additional model families/scales in the appendices.

#### Evaluation scope

The paper covers 12 benchmark tasks, including SingleEq, AddSub, MultiArith, GSM8K, AQuA-RAT, SVAMP, CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA, date understanding, tracking shuffled objects, last-letter concatenation, and coin-flip reasoning.

#### Concrete results

- On **MultiArith**, Zero-shot-CoT improves accuracy from **17.7% to 78.7%** relative to standard zero-shot prompting.
- On **GSM8K**, it improves from **10.4% to 40.7%** with the main InstructGPT setup.
- With **PaLM 540B**, GSM8K improves **12.5%→43.0%**, MultiArith **25.5%→66.1%**, and AQuA-RAT **23.4%→36.1%**.
- Combining PaLM Zero-shot-CoT with self-consistency (40 paths) raises GSM8K to **70.1%** and MultiArith to **89.0%**, showing complementarity between elicitation and multi-path decoding.

#### Prompt and scale sensitivity

- The paper tests multiple reasoning triggers and finds material variation between phrases; “Let’s think step by step” is the strongest among those studied.
- Gains emerge mainly in sufficiently large models. Smaller GPT-2/GPT-Neo/GPT-J-scale models do not exhibit the same robust zero-shot reasoning effect.
- The authors' analysis emphasizes that the trigger is not a task solver by itself; it exposes reasoning capability already latent in the pretrained model.

#### Error analysis

The paper manually analyzes generated chains of thought and finds that correct final answers can coexist with flawed reasoning and vice versa. On CommonsenseQA, among sampled correct predictions, **22%** contained an incorrect chain of thought. Incorrect predictions were attributed mainly to commonsense mistakes and logical mistakes. Arithmetic outputs can also continue reasoning after reaching the correct value and subsequently change it to a wrong answer.

#### Limitations and boundaries

- Performance is **prompt-phrase sensitive**; a generic reasoning cue is not invariant across wording.
- The effect depends strongly on **model scale**.
- Generated reasoning is not guaranteed to be faithful or logically valid; a correct final answer does not prove the rationale is correct.
- The two-stage answer-extraction procedure depends on task-specific output-format handling.
- The authors note that web-trained LLMs inherit/amplify biases present in training data; prompting does not remove those shortcomings.
- The benchmarks are mostly static reasoning tasks, not interactive browser environments with changing state or external tools.

#### Relevance to LLM agents

Zero-shot-CoT is relevant to agent planning because it demonstrates that explicit reasoning can be elicited without curated demonstrations for every new task. However, web agents cannot treat generated CoT as verified state: plans and rationales must be checked against the actual browser/tool environment. The paper therefore supports reasoning elicitation, not trustworthy execution.

#### Synthesis statement

Zero-shot-CoT demonstrates that a generic reasoning trigger can expose strong multi-step reasoning in large pretrained models—for example 10.4%→40.7% on GSM8K and 17.7%→78.7% on MultiArith—but the effect is scale- and wording-sensitive and the generated rationale can itself be wrong, so an agent must pair reasoning traces with external verification.
