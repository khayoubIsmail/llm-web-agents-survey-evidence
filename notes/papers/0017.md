- **Authors:** Mike Lewis, Yinhan Liu, Naman Goyal, Marjan Ghazvininejad, Abdelrahman Mohamed, Omer Levy, Ves Stoyanov, Luke Zettlemoyer
- **Venue:** ACL 2020
- **Pages:** 7871–7880
- **DOI:** 10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.703
- **Survey role:** P2 foundation for encoder–decoder pretrained models, generation, reconstruction, and later agent summarization/transformation components

### Research problem

BART asks how to pretrain a sequence-to-sequence Transformer so that one architecture works well for both language understanding and free-form generation. Earlier pretrained models tended to specialize: BERT-style masked encoders are strong for bidirectional understanding but are awkward for autoregressive generation, whereas GPT-style left-to-right decoders generate naturally but cannot condition each token representation on future input context.

BART combines a **bidirectional encoder** with a **left-to-right autoregressive decoder** and trains the pair as a denoising autoencoder: corrupt a document using an arbitrary noising function, then reconstruct the original sequence. This makes the pretraining interface naturally compatible with tasks where an input must be transformed into an output sequence.

### Architecture

The model uses the standard Transformer sequence-to-sequence architecture. The base version has **6 encoder and 6 decoder layers**; the large version has **12 layers in each**. Decoder layers include cross-attention to the encoder output. Relative to an equivalently sized BERT, the paper reports roughly **10% more parameters** because of the decoder/cross-attention structure.

For large-scale experiments, BART-large uses hidden size 1024, batch size **8,000**, and **500,000 pretraining steps**. It uses GPT-2 byte-pair encoding and the same approximately **160 GB** mixture of news, books, stories, and web text used for RoBERTa. Thirty percent of tokens are masked in the selected denoising configuration.

### Corruption/noising objectives

The paper evaluates five input transformations, which can also be composed:

- **Token masking:** replace sampled tokens with `[MASK]`.
- **Token deletion:** remove sampled tokens entirely, forcing the model to infer both content and missing positions.
- **Text infilling:** sample variable-length spans from a Poisson distribution with λ=3 and replace each complete span with a single mask token; zero-length spans correspond to mask insertions.
- **Sentence permutation:** split a document into sentences and shuffle their order.
- **Document rotation:** choose a random token as the apparent beginning of the document.

The large model ultimately combines **text infilling + sentence permutation**. The key methodological advantage is that the encoder input and decoder output do not need to have aligned token positions or lengths, so arbitrary document corruption can be used.

### Controlled comparison of pretraining objectives

A major strength of the paper is the controlled objective study. Base-size models are trained for **1 million steps on the same data** and evaluated under substantially matched architecture/fine-tuning conditions. The comparison includes BERT-like masked language modeling, MASS-like masked sequence-to-sequence training, GPT-like language modeling, XLNet-inspired permuted language modeling, UniLM-style multitask masking, and several BART noising schemes.

The results show that no single objective dominates every task. A pure left-to-right language model has the best ELI5 perplexity in this controlled experiment but very poor SQuAD performance. Document rotation and sentence shuffling perform badly when used alone. Successful objectives generally include token deletion/masking or attention masking, and **left-to-right autoregressive pretraining is particularly useful for generation**.

Text-infilling BART is the most consistently strong configuration. In Table 1, BART with text infilling reaches **90.8 SQuAD F1** and **84.0 MNLI accuracy**, while generation perplexities are 24.26 on ELI5, **6.61 on XSum**, **11.05 on ConvAI2**, and **5.83 on CNN/DailyMail**. Combining text infilling with sentence shuffling keeps SQuAD at 90.8, MNLI at 83.8, and improves CNN/DailyMail perplexity further to **5.41**.

The authors explicitly caution that the pretraining objective is not the only determinant of performance: architectural differences such as relative positional embeddings and segment recurrence help explain why their simplified reproduction of a permuted language model trails the published XLNet result.

### Discriminative-task results

Despite being designed to support generation, BART remains competitive on standard understanding benchmarks. On GLUE, BART-large is broadly comparable to RoBERTa and XLNet. Representative BART results include:

- MNLI **89.9/90.1** matched/mismatched accuracy;
- SST-2 **96.6**;
- QQP **92.5**;
- QNLI **94.9**;
- RTE **87.0**;
- MRPC **90.4**;
- CoLA **62.8**.

On SQuAD, BART scores **88.8 EM / 94.6 F1** on SQuAD 1.1 and **86.1 / 89.2** on SQuAD 2.0, essentially matching RoBERTa's F1 values. The important conclusion is that adding an autoregressive decoder and optimizing for denoising/generation does not require sacrificing competitive bidirectional comprehension.

### Summarization results

Generation is where the paper reports its strongest gains. On CNN/DailyMail, BART obtains **ROUGE-1 44.16, ROUGE-2 21.28, ROUGE-L 40.90**. On the more abstractive XSum benchmark it obtains **45.14 / 22.27 / 37.25**, outperforming the strongest comparison in the table by roughly **3.5 ROUGE points** across the XSum metrics.

Human evaluation on XSum provides a useful qualification. Annotators prefer BART over BERTSumExtAbs **73% versus 27%**, but prefer the human reference **67% versus 33%** over BART. Thus the automated improvement is substantial without implying human-level summarization.

### Dialogue and abstractive QA

On ConvAI2 dialogue response generation, BART reports **20.72 validation F1** and **11.85 validation perplexity**, improving over the prior system shown in the paper (19.09 F1 / 17.51 PPL).

On ELI5 long-form abstractive question answering, BART reaches **ROUGE-1 30.6, ROUGE-2 6.2, ROUGE-L 24.3**. Its ROUGE-L improves over the strongest listed prior result by about **1.2 points**. The paper notes that ELI5 remains unusually difficult because the answer is only weakly constrained by the input documents; this is also the task where a pure language-model objective was strongest in the controlled pretraining experiment.

### Machine translation

The paper also uses BART as a pretrained target-side model for WMT16 Romanian→English translation. A newly initialized source encoder learns to map Romanian into representations that the pretrained English BART can denoise/generate. With back-translation data, the baseline Transformer scores **36.80 BLEU**, fixed-BART training scores 36.29, and fully tuned BART reaches **37.96**, a **+1.16 BLEU** improvement over the strong baseline (reported in the paper as approximately +1.1 BLEU).

The authors report that preliminary experiments **without back-translation were less effective and prone to overfitting**, identifying additional regularization as future work. This qualification is important: the translation gain is not evidence that target-language-only BART pretraining is universally sufficient.

### Qualitative analysis and hallucination

The qualitative XSum examples show fluent, highly abstractive summaries that combine information from different parts of the source. However, the analysis also identifies a concrete factuality failure: BART can insert plausible background information that is **unsupported by the document**. The authors explicitly characterize the model's main qualitative limitation as a tendency to **hallucinate unsupported information**.

This is particularly relevant to schema-guided data extraction. A model optimized for fluent transformation can produce an attractive answer while inventing fields or unsupported provenance. Generation quality therefore cannot substitute for field-level evidence checking.

### Critical assessment and limitations

BART is a foundational sequence-to-sequence pretraining paper, not a web-agent paper. Its strongest contribution to this survey is architectural: a pretrained encoder-decoder can jointly support input understanding and conditional generation, which later systems reuse for summarization, transformation, extraction, dialogue, and action/text generation.

Important limitations are:

- The experiments are static NLP tasks; there is no browser interaction, action grounding, state tracking, tool use, or long-horizon recovery.
- The large model is pretrained on a substantial 160 GB corpus and 500k large-batch steps, so the results do not isolate data/compute efficiency.
- The controlled objective comparison simplifies some competing methods, and the authors themselves state that architectural details beyond the objective can materially change results.
- The ELI5 results show that BART is not universally the best objective when outputs are only loosely conditioned on inputs.
- Summarization generations can hallucinate unsupported information, directly limiting their suitability for evidence-preserving extraction without verification.
- The Romanian→English approach relies on back-translation; without it the method overfits in preliminary experiments.
- The paper leaves exploration of new or task-specific corruption schemes to future work rather than establishing that text infilling + sentence permutation is universally optimal.

### Relevance to generalized web automation and extraction

For this survey, BART is supporting evidence for the evolution of models capable of both encoding source context and generating structured or transformed outputs. Its denoising formulation is conceptually related to reconstructing desired information from noisy or incomplete input, and its encoder-decoder architecture became important for downstream summarization and information transformation.

However, BART also illustrates why **generation quality is not evidence integrity**. The paper's own hallucination examples justify separate validation/provenance mechanisms when generative models are used for data extraction. A web extraction agent must not only produce fluent records; it must anchor each field to observable source evidence and distinguish reconstruction from invention.
