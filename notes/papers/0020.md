### Paper-specific synthesis

#### Question and method

Hoffmann et al. ask how model parameters and training tokens should be allocated under a fixed training-compute budget. They study more than 400 language models ranging from roughly 70M to 16B parameters and 5B–500B training tokens, using three complementary empirical approaches: fixing compute and varying model size, fitting parametric loss functions over model/data scale, and analyzing IsoFLOP curves.

#### Main finding

The central result is that many large language models were **undertrained**: model size had grown faster than the amount of training data. Under the authors' compute-optimal prescription, parameter count and number of training tokens should grow approximately equally with additional compute, rather than allocating most extra compute to parameters.

To test this prediction, they train **Chinchilla**, a 70B-parameter model on about **1.4 trillion tokens**, using approximately the same training compute as the 280B-parameter Gopher, which used about 300B tokens.

#### Concrete results

Chinchilla outperforms Gopher and other larger contemporary models on a broad range of language-modeling and downstream evaluations while using substantially fewer parameters at inference. The paper reports an average **67.5% accuracy on MMLU**, compared with **60.0% for Gopher** in its evaluation table, alongside lower perplexity on language-modeling benchmarks. The result demonstrates that data allocation can dominate raw parameter count at fixed compute.

#### Relevance to this survey

This is enabling rather than direct agent evidence. Web agents inherit the cost, latency, context-processing and inference characteristics of their base LLMs. Chinchilla shows that effective model capability is a function of both parameter scale and training-data scale, and that smaller compute-optimally trained backbones can outperform larger undertrained models. This is relevant when comparing agent systems whose base models differ in training regime and serving cost.

#### Limitations

The scaling laws are empirical fits to the model/data/training regime studied by DeepMind and need not transfer unchanged to later architectures, mixture-of-experts models, instruction tuning, multimodal training or retrieval-augmented agents. The paper evaluates language modeling and NLP benchmarks, not interactive Web automation. Compute-optimal pretraining also does not imply inference-optimal deployment because serving cost and memory depend strongly on parameter count.

#### Evidence locations

Scaling methodology: Sections 2–3. Chinchilla construction: Section 4. Downstream/language-modeling comparisons: Section 4 result tables. Discussion of compute-optimal training and implications: conclusion/discussion.

#### One-sentence synthesis

The Chinchilla study shows that, at fixed training compute, balancing parameter growth with substantially more training data can produce a smaller model that outperforms much larger undertrained models, an important systems consideration for agent backbones but not direct evidence about Web-agent behavior.
