## Analysis

<!-- note-body-start -->
### Research problem and contribution

PlanBench tests whether language models can plan from explicit action models rather than rely on memorized commonsense scripts. It translates PDDL-style planning domains into controlled natural language and provides an extensible curriculum covering plan generation, optimality, verification, execution reasoning, replanning, generalization, reuse, and robustness to surface-form changes.

### Method and evaluation

Instances are generated from classical planning domains, principally Blocksworld and Logistics, with executable plans checked against formal domain definitions. One-shot prompts give a worked example before asking GPT-4 or InstructGPT-3 to solve a new instance. On 600 Blocksworld instances, GPT-4 solves 206 plan-generation cases (34.3%), 198 optimal-planning cases (33.0%), and 352 plan-verification cases (58.6%); performance also degrades on state prediction, replanning, and shuffled predicate descriptions. Because the underlying transition system is known, invalid actions and non-goal-reaching plans can be distinguished from formatting errors.

### Limitations and critical assessment

The benchmark deliberately abstracts away perception, partial observability, stochastic transitions, and tool failures, so it tests symbolic planning competence rather than complete web-agent performance. Natural-language templates may create parsing artifacts and favor models familiar with PDDL-like phrasing. The main model comparison is narrow and now historically dated, and one-shot prompting is not a comparison with tool-augmented solvers or constrained decoding. Exact plan correctness is appropriate for formal domains but does not capture useful partial progress. These limits make PlanBench diagnostic of a planning bottleneck, not a ceiling on systems that combine an LLM with an external planner.

### Relevance to the survey

PlanBench supports the claim that fluent reasoning is not equivalent to reliable action planning. For web agents, its main contribution is methodological: use formally checkable transitions and perturbations to separate genuine planning from memorized task patterns, while recognizing that live-web uncertainty adds further challenges.
