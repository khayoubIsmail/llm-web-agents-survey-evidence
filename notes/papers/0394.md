### Research problem

The paper targets a deployment obstacle that many web-agent benchmarks deliberately avoid: **interactive CAPTCHAs**. The authors argue that real commercial websites often contain CAPTCHA systems, whereas benchmark construction commonly filters such pages out. This creates an evaluation blind spot because agents that succeed on CAPTCHA-free benchmark sites may still be unable to complete the same workflow on a real site.

Open CaptchaWorld therefore reframes CAPTCHA solving as a **multistep multimodal-agent task** rather than traditional distorted-text recognition. The benchmark requires perception, planning, memory, and interaction in a live browser loop through actions such as clicking, dragging, typing, and submitting.

### Benchmark design

Open CaptchaWorld contains **20 modern interactive CAPTCHA types**. Traditional distorted-text recognition and static classification tasks are intentionally excluded because simple detection/classification models can already address many of them. The selected tasks instead include interaction-heavy forms such as image matching, object matching, sequence clicking, sliders, rotation matching, path finding, dot placement, hold-button tasks, counting, and other visually grounded puzzles.

The paper states that each CAPTCHA type has **at least 10 diverse variants**, created by changing spatial layouts, icons, interaction modes, viewing angles, object positions, or contextual cues. Images are either designed by humans or generated with GPT-4o. Human annotators then provide task instructions, reasoning-depth annotations, and final ground-truth solutions.

The benchmark is designed so that tasks are generally easy for humans but difficult for multimodal agents; it intentionally emphasizes interactive visual-cognitive difficulty rather than broad external knowledge.

### CAPTCHA Reasoning Depth

The paper introduces **CAPTCHA Reasoning Depth**, a task-agnostic complexity measure defined as the minimum number of atomic reasoning or interaction steps needed to solve a CAPTCHA. Atomic steps are drawn from visual, cognitive, motor-control, and state-monitoring categories. Examples include recognizing an object, counting items, planning an ordering, remembering a cue, dragging an element, clicking a target, and checking interface feedback.

Human annotators solve sample puzzles while decomposing their process into atomic steps using a structured checklist. The paper also prompts OpenAI-o3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro to estimate reasoning depth under the same guidelines. Across the dataset, the reported **mean human reasoning depth is 2.94 with standard deviation 0.92**.

A qualitative comparison reveals a systematic difference between human and model reasoning. Humans compress familiar low-level operations into a few goal-directed steps, whereas the model tends to **over-segment or overthink**, explicitly counting perception, memorization, each click, and progress checks as separate reasoning units. The authors interpret this as evidence of a gap in abstraction, intuition, memory reuse, and filtering of irrelevant details.

### Dataset curation pipeline

The curation pipeline has four stages:

1. **Type-related image curation:** brainstorm/search for 20 CAPTCHA types and create visual variants with human design or GPT-4o generation.
2. **CAPTCHA generation:** turn those images into interactive browser tasks with diverse instructions.
3. **Reasoning-depth estimation:** annotate the atomic cognitive and interaction steps required to solve each task.
4. **Final annotation:** verify instructions and ground-truth solutions so the benchmark remains human-solvable.

The paper describes the benchmark as an open-source, web-based test environment intended for continued extension rather than a fixed static image dataset.

### Agent formulation and interaction loop

CAPTCHA solving is modeled as a finite-horizon **partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP)**. The latent state corresponds to the current CAPTCHA configuration; observations are screenshots; actions include clicks and drags; transitions update the interface; and reward corresponds to success or failure. The discount factor is set to 1 because CAPTCHA types are weighted equally.

The Browser-Use agents operate in closed loop: observe a screenshot, reason about the current task/state, choose an action, observe the resulting state, and continue until submission. This is more representative of web automation than single-shot visual QA because an incorrect early action can alter the subsequent interaction state.

### Evaluated models and protocol

The paper evaluates Browser-Use agents with multiple MLLM backbones in a zero-shot setting across all 20 CAPTCHA types. The evaluated models include GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, OpenAI-o3, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, OpenAI-o1, and DeepSeek-V3.

The main metric is **Pass@1 / success rate** for completing the CAPTCHA sequence. The paper also reports the monetary evaluation cost associated with each backbone, enabling a cost-versus-success comparison.

### Main quantitative results

Human participants achieve **93.3% Pass@1**. Every tested browser-use agent is far below this level. Table 1 reports:

- **OpenAI-o3:** 40.0% success, cost $66.4.
- **GPT-4.1:** 25.0%, cost $16.7.
- **Gemini 2.5 Pro:** 25.0%, cost $18.1.
- **Claude 3.7 Sonnet:** 20.0%, cost $18.7.
- **DeepSeek-V3:** 20.0%, cost $7.3.
- **Claude 3.5 Haiku:** 15.0%, cost $9.3.
- **Claude 3.5 Sonnet:** 10.0%, cost $21.9.
- **GPT-4o:** 5.7%, cost $25.8.
- **OpenAI-o1:** 5.0%, cost $94.6.

The human-to-best-agent gap is therefore **53.3 percentage points**. The cost results also show that stronger reasoning does not automatically imply cost-effective deployment. OpenAI-o3 gives the best success rate but at substantial cost, while OpenAI-o1 is the least cost-effective in this table, with only 5% success despite the highest reported cost.

### CAPTCHA-type capability analysis

The appendix provides a per-type support matrix indicating whether each model solved at least one instance of each CAPTCHA type. Basic visual tasks such as Image Recognition, Image Matching, and Select Animal are solved by many models. Interaction-heavy or more compositional tasks remain much harder.

The paper reports that **Slide Puzzle, Dice Count, Place Dot, Connect Icon, Click Order, Misleading Click, Pick Area**, and several other difficult types are unsolved or nearly unsolved by the tested agents; the exact support matrix exposes considerable variation across model families. OpenAI-o3 shows the broadest coverage, including successes on Patch Select, Dart Count, and Rotation Match. Claude 3.7 Sonnet shows isolated strengths on Bingo-type tasks and Hold Button.

This is important because aggregate success alone hides whether a system fails due to perception, arithmetic, spatial manipulation, stateful control, or sequencing.

### Failure analysis

The paper groups representative OpenAI-o3 failures into **three concrete patterns**:

1. **Correct strategy, inadequate perception/spatial grounding:** the agent understands what should happen but clicks the wrong location, as in dot-placement tasks.
2. **Correct goal, failed fine-grained operational execution:** the agent understands a slider/alignment objective but cannot calculate or execute the precise movement needed.
3. **Misguided solution strategy based on irrelevant cues:** the agent relies on filenames, HTML text, or other spurious information instead of solving the visual task.

These categories separate high-level reasoning failure from visual grounding and motor/action execution failure, making the benchmark diagnostically useful for web-agent evaluation.

### Cost-performance implications

The paper explicitly plots evaluation cost against Pass@1. OpenAI-o3 offers the highest success but with high cost; Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek-V3 provide more favorable cost/performance trade-offs while remaining far below human success. The authors argue that robust and cost-effective interactive reasoning remains an open problem.

For the survey, this supports treating **cost as an independent evaluation dimension** rather than assuming the most accurate model is necessarily the most practical agent.

### Critical assessment and limitations

Open CaptchaWorld is useful because it introduces a class of obstacles that conventional web benchmarks often remove, thereby exposing a real deployment boundary. Its use of closed-loop browser interaction also allows failures to propagate across steps instead of reducing the task to image classification.

Important limitations are:

- The benchmark is intentionally modest in scale: 20 CAPTCHA types with at least 10 variants each are diverse but do not cover the full ecosystem of commercial CAPTCHA systems or adversarial anti-bot mechanisms.
- Many images are human-designed or GPT-4o-generated simulations inspired by commercial CAPTCHA styles; they are not measurements of actual production CAPTCHA providers.
- Success on this benchmark should not be interpreted as authorization or capability to bypass real services. Its value for this survey is diagnostic evaluation of multimodal interaction and robustness.
- The reasoning-depth metric relies on decomposing human/model behavior into atomic steps, so the exact count depends on annotation granularity. The paper itself demonstrates this sensitivity by showing that models over-segment the same task relative to humans.
- The model comparison is tied to a particular Browser-Use scaffold and 2025 model versions, so later scaffolds or specialized agents may change absolute scores.
- Pass@1 captures final completion but does not by itself measure trajectory efficiency, action validity, provenance, or safety; the failure analysis is needed to interpret why an episode failed.

### Relevance to this survey

Open CaptchaWorld supports the survey's argument that **generalized web automation requires robust interaction under obstacles that are absent from sanitized benchmark environments**. It also offers direct evidence that high-level multimodal reasoning and visual QA performance do not guarantee precise stateful interaction.

For WADE-style evaluation, the paper motivates at least four dimensions: end-to-end task success, interaction/grounding correctness, reasoning/trajectory complexity, and cost. Its three-way failure analysis is also useful when separating perception errors, operational errors, and planning/strategy errors.
