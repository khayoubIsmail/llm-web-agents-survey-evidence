- **Venue:** Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2025

### Research problem

X-WebAgentBench tests a weakness largely hidden by English-only web benchmarks: whether an agent can understand instructions and interact with an interface when both the **instruction language and web environment language vary**. The authors argue that high English WebShop results overestimate practical global-agent capability and report an English-to-multilingual performance gap exceeding 20% in their motivating comparison.

### Benchmark composition

The benchmark is derived from WebShop but rebuilt as a multilingual interactive e-commerce environment. It contains:

- **14 languages** spanning **7 language families**;
- **2,800 task instructions**, with **200 final validated instructions per language**;
- **589,946 multilingual products** across **13 product categories**;
- **6 distinct environment actions**;
- an average decision space of about **211 products per instruction** after the environment is simplified to control context length.

The 14 languages are Chinese, French, Spanish, German, Greek, Bulgarian, Russian, Turkish, Arabic, Vietnamese, Thai, Hindi, Swahili, and Urdu, with English retained as the source/reference environment. The benchmark explicitly supports both multilingual instructions and multilingual interactive environments, unlike the English-only comparison benchmarks in its Table 1.

### Construction and quality control

The construction process has four stages: data preparation, multilingual-instruction construction, multilingual-environment construction, and quality checking. The authors begin by randomly selecting **500 informative WebShop instructions** and manually removing ambiguous requests.

For instruction translation, they pre-validate automated translation on **50 English instructions across all 14 languages** with three expert judges. Google Translate exceeds **90% accuracy across all languages** in this check and is selected over prompted GPT-4 for the large-scale translation step, especially because GPT-4 is less reliable in lower-resource languages.

Quality control then combines annotator onboarding, LLM checking, and manual rechecking. Annotators must score at least **80** on a 50-item onboarding test. GPT-4 rates translation equivalence on a 0–10 scale; only items scoring at least **8** survive the automated check. Approximately 250 candidates per language remain before final manual recheck, which yields **200 high-quality instructions per language**. The paper reports **Cohen's kappa = 0.8** for the manual recheck process.

### Evaluation protocol

The principal metric is WebShop-style **Task Score**, which gives partial credit based on how well the selected product satisfies the requested attributes rather than using only binary success. Five LLMs are evaluated: Mistral-7B-Instruct, Llama3-8B, Qwen2-7B-Instruct, GPT-3.5-turbo, and GPT-4o.

For each model, four strategies are compared:

1. **BaseAgent:** interacts directly in the local language.
2. **Translate-en:** translates the multilingual environment to English using Google Translate.
3. **Self-Translate-en:** asks the LLM itself to translate into English.
4. **CLP:** cross-lingual alignment prompting followed by task-solving prompts.

The paper later evaluates ReAct, BOLAA, local/cross-lingual demonstrations, a translation tool, and reasoning-oriented DeepSeek models to diagnose the source of multilingual failures.

### Main quantitative results

Performance is low even for strong models. Across 14 languages, GPT-4o's BaseAgent average Task Score is **37.11**. Adding CLP raises the average slightly to **37.94**, while Translate-en falls to 29.03 and Self-Translate-en to 22.06. For GPT-3.5-turbo, BaseAgent scores 26.52 on average and CLP improves it to **29.79**. Smaller models benefit more from external English translation: Qwen2-7B rises from **15.08** with BaseAgent to **18.75** with Translate-en, while Llama3-8B rises from 4.70 to **8.53** and Mistral-7B from 3.51 to **4.45**.

These results support two nuanced findings rather than a generic statement that multilingual agents are weak. First, **cross-lingual alignment strategies help mainly when the underlying model already has substantial multilingual capacity**. Second, for weaker models, translating the environment to English can be more useful than expecting the model to perform its own cross-lingual alignment.

### Action and token-efficiency analysis

The benchmark records interaction trajectories, allowing the authors to analyze action overuse. Correct behavior typically requires around **3–4 clicks** and **1–2 searches**, but several lower-resource or less frequently represented languages induce substantially more actions. For example, German and Turkish cause elevated click counts for Qwen2-7B, while Thai and Hindi lead GPT-family agents to overuse search actions.

Token cost is also uneven across languages. The paper reports that several non-Latin-script languages, particularly Greek and Hindi, consume **at least about twice as many tokens as English** in the studied setup. The authors interpret this as a form of multilingual token-cost unfairness that can make global agent deployment more expensive even when task quality is similar.

### Long-horizon degradation

A key agentic finding is that reward/task performance is concentrated in early interaction steps and drops sharply after roughly **five actions**, while the authors state that human experts often need **more than ten action steps** in X-WebAgentBench. This indicates that multilingual interaction compounds the familiar long-horizon reliability problem: current agents degrade before completing trajectories of realistic length.

### Cross-lingual demonstrations

The authors compare English, German, and local-language in-context demonstrations for ReAct. Contrary to the expectation that a high-resource cross-lingual demonstration would transfer well, **local-language demonstrations outperform English/German demonstrations in most tested languages**. Increasing the number of demonstrations helps mid- and low-resource languages more consistently than high-resource languages, where gains peak and can decline after additional shots.

BOLAA with local-language demonstrations improves over single-agent ReAct, but the multilingual scores still remain below English performance. Thus stronger agent scaffolding narrows but does not remove the language gap.

### Translation-tool non-use

The authors add an explicit translation action to the agent's available tools to test whether models recognize their own multilingual limitations. Across all **14 languages, the tested LLMs never choose the translation function**. The paper interprets this as overconfidence or inadequate tool-selection awareness: an available capability is useless if the agent fails to recognize when it should invoke it.

This result is particularly relevant to tool-using web agents because it separates **tool availability** from **tool-use policy quality**.

### Reasoning versus language alignment

To test whether poor multilingual web interaction is simply a reasoning problem, the authors evaluate DeepSeek-R1 and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B on six representative languages. Their Task Scores remain **below 50**, and DeepSeek-R1 performs similarly to GPT-4o rather than decisively solving the benchmark. The authors conclude that the principal bottleneck is **cross-lingual alignment**, not merely general reasoning strength.

### Critical assessment and limitations

X-WebAgentBench provides unusually direct evidence that web-agent generalization across sites is not enough if language variation is ignored. Its controlled WebShop base makes cross-language comparisons much cleaner than comparing unrelated national websites.

However, several limits matter for survey interpretation:

- The benchmark is fundamentally an **e-commerce/WebShop-derived** environment; results should not be generalized automatically to forms, enterprise software, search engines, or arbitrary live websites.
- Most multilingual content is machine-translated. The authors explicitly acknowledge that Google Translate plus quality checks cannot reproduce the quality and cultural naturalness of experienced human translation.
- The environment is intentionally simplified to an average of 211 products per instruction to control context length, so it is not the full scale or volatility of open-web commerce.
- The 200 final instructions per language provide controlled coverage but remain a limited sample of linguistic phenomena and user intents.
- Task Score measures product-attribute satisfaction and does not independently score provenance, safety, or every trajectory error.

### Relevance to this survey

This paper is useful evidence for **generalization and robustness** in web agents. A system that works on an English benchmark may fail under semantically equivalent instructions and interfaces in another language. It also demonstrates that multilingual failures manifest not only as lower final task scores but as **higher token cost, action overuse, premature long-horizon degradation, and failure to invoke an available translation tool**.

For WADE-style evaluation, the benchmark motivates language as an explicit perturbation/generalization axis and supports tracking task score together with action efficiency, tool-selection behavior, and cost.
