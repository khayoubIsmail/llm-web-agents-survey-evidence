## Analysis

<!-- note-body-start -->
### Research problem and contribution

This paper directly tests whether an LLM can replace a programmed text-game transition function. It introduces ByteSized32-State-Prediction, built from state/action/next-state transitions and game rules across 31 text games, and evaluates both complete next-state generation and a state-difference representation.

### Method and evaluation

GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 receive the current JSON state, an action, contextual rules or demonstrations, and must predict the next state. The balanced evaluation distinguishes static transitions from transitions that change state, and action-driven changes from environment-driven dynamics. GPT-4 reaches at most 77.1% accuracy on dynamic action-driven transitions but only 49.7% on dynamic environment-driven transitions. Difference prediction helps some dynamic cases by reducing output burden, while full-state generation can better preserve unchanged information. In a deliberately sampled human comparison, annotators average 80% versus GPT-4's 49%, showing that the benchmark is difficult but not inherently ambiguous. The paper notes multiplicative long-horizon error: even a 90% accurate one-step model has only about 35% probability of ten error-free steps.

### Limitations and critical assessment

The study evaluates only two proprietary model families and two JSON encodings. Games emphasize commonsense and elementary scientific dynamics, so results may not transfer to visual interfaces, stochastic environments, or domains with hidden external state. Exact state comparison penalizes harmless serialization differences but is necessary for simulator fidelity. Conversely, high one-step accuracy can still mask compounding rollout errors, and the experiments do not close the loop by planning inside the learned simulator. The authors also warn that generated transitions can be unsafe or nonsensical and should not directly mediate human-facing applications.

### Relevance to the survey

The results caution against treating an LLM as a dependable web world model without calibration and live-state verification. Model-based planning may be useful for proposing branches, but small transition errors compound rapidly; action-driven UI changes are also likely easier to predict than asynchronous, server-driven, or cross-user environmental changes.
